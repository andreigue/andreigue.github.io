### *The Stranger, by Albert Camus*

I’ve written before about [watches](https://andreigue.github.io/some-thoughts/), and how I think that they are an extreme interest to have. But precisely because it is such an “edge-case” interest, understanding it made other interests easier to understand.

Meursault, the main character, embodies an extreme mindset in the same way a luxury watch is an extreme interest. Usually extremes are not good to live by, but seeing it in action gets the point across better. Once you understand the extreme, you become better equipped to understand all the milder versions of it on the spectrum. So although it may seem at points in this article that I am praising Meursault’s coldness, I am not. I am interested in the less extreme version of what he reveals.

Now I was going to leave the parallel between watches and The Stranger at that, but writing this, I just realized that there is an actual deep parallel between them.

## Bare Reality is Relatively Empty

Imagine you get promoted after years at a company. Your salary jumps, your status changes, and to mark the occasion, you buy yourself a $15,000 Rolex.

On paper, this is absurd. A $50 quartz watch tells time more accurately. But the two are not the same.

The Rolex just so happens to be a physical item known as a "watch" that a lot of people place in the same category as a $50 Casio. But most of the Rolex's value is not derived from the physical layer of reality.

Yes, it consists of a stainless steel case, a sapphire crystal protecting the dial, and a mechanical movement of meticulously assembled gears, springs, jewels, and screws, some so small you could lift them only with the moisture of your fingertip.

So, you can find beauty staying solely within the confines of the physical realm.

However, you'd then miss everything that cannot be found inside the watch:

It is now your reminder of how far you've come. What you've achieved. It now reminds you that \$15'000 is nothing to you. I mean, there it is sitting on your wrist all day, getting accidentally brushed against hard surfaces and picking up light scratches. It's not like you were planning to resell it anyway. You don't need that money back. You don't want that money back. Your whole paradigm of money has changed. What you used to believe was expensive is now reachable. And maybe, because of that, you carry yourself a little differently too. You enter a room full of new people, and before you even open your mouth, something has already been communicated, whether through the watch itself or through the quiet confidence it gives you.

And then there is the memory attached to it. The fleeting moments that you call "my promotion", or "my promotion day". The joyful ride back home. The stupid smile you kept trying to hide at red lights. Greeting your spouse and kids and sharing the good news. Going to a fancy restaurant and ordering a Tomahawk steak for the first time in your life. The waiter opening the bottle of wine, and the feeling that life had opened up with it. The quiet trails of perfume claiming the space between tables. All of the aforementioned memories, along with pride, relief, disbelief, ambition, identity, and the feeling that the world had become wider, get cemented into this one object, which just so happens to be a watch.

So clearly, there is more to it than cold steel and soulless gears spinning.

But it's all fake. It's not real. It's not there. As Matthew McConaughey said in The Wolf of Wall Street:

> *"Fugayzi, fugazi. It's a whazy. It's a woozie. It's fairy dust. It doesn't exist. It's never landed. It is no matter. It's not on the elemental chart. It's not fucking real.”*

And that's what a lot of human existence is.

It's stories and feelings that we attach to objects, events, jobs, relationships, places, titles, and situations.

Strip away that added layer, and the world becomes an open, relatively empty field.

And that's how Meursault, the stranger, lives his life.

He only sees the literal version of reality, whereas everyone else sees the enriched version.

He describes his mother’s death with the same flat attention he gives to drinking coffee, feeling sunlight on his shoulder, or feeling sand caught beneath the toenail of his big toe. He does not add layers of meaning onto anything. Nothing gets the privilege of being elevated. Nothing gets pushed into the sacred, the tragic, or the heroic. Everything stays on the same level.

It's kind of analogous to animals. If a lion is hungry, he'll go hunt and eat. Tired, sleep. Attracted, mate. Threatened, react. Hot, seek shade. It's not complicated. They don't ask questions like “What is the purpose of my life?”, “Am I successful?” or “What will society think of me?”.

### Finding Common Ground with Absurdism

While this book is my formal introduction to absurdism, I have inadvertently been living around these ideas for years through philosophies such as Stoicism and Buddhism.

As I said in the beginning, Meursault represents absurdism taken to an extreme. But that mental framework also comes with a lot of qualities I appreciate. For example, he lives in the moment. He doesn't dwell on past decisions. He doesn't fantasize about the future. He doesn't build grand life narratives. He doesn't fret over things outside his control (although he also doesn't fret over things within his control). It doesn't even cross his mind what others think of him. He accepts that there is no given roadmap on how to live life. He accepts the universe's complete indifference to humankind.

There are probably others that I appreciate, but I am on a time limit as I write these thoughts, and I don't want to spend too long writing this article, and one of my philosophies right now is to finish things, regardless of whether its perfect or not. That's me training detachment of the outcome.

There are probably other ideas that I appreciate, but I'm on a time limit as I write these thoughts. One of the principles I've been trying to live by lately is to finish things, even if they're imperfect. Writing this article is another opportunity to practice that. It's me training detachment from the outcome.

And that's a Buddhist concept. Buddhism says that attachment, in any form, is a major source of suffering. The moment you say anything remotely close to "I am a successful person because I own this watch", or, "the quality of this article is a direct reflection of who I am", you've created a source of potential suffering. Because what if one day, the economy crashes and you get fired from your job. What if someone thinks this article is complete garbage with zero substance.

Those are real risks. And if you benefit from the human element enough to justify those risks, then to a certain extent, creating and adding the human layer to things is worth it. It's what makes life worth living. How can you deeply love someone and not be attached to them? I'm not sure.

And if having a \$15'000 Rolex will make you walk straighter, with your chest up and your voice louder and more confident, even if it is all in your head, that confidence will eventually lead to real-world results in the form of new opportunities. Because the snowball effect is real. The halo effect is real. The placebo effect is real. And so it is not unwise to embrace this human tendency to attach meaning to things. But I think it's important to remain aware of it.

It reminds me of leverage in finance. One reason the West has grown so much economically is its willingness to use credit. We are comfortable borrowing money today to fund projects that we expect will create more value tomorrow. However leverage works in both directions. It amplifies both gains and losses. A prime example is the 2008 financial crisis. American banks were more willing to take on risk, while Canadian banks were generally more conservative. When the cracks started to show, the U.S. economy suffered much more than Canada’s.

Attaching meaning to things is similar. The more meaning you attach to something, the more joy it will bring. But the more it will hurt when times get tough.

And everyone's tendencies and risk tolerance are different. Some cannot help but deeply love and feel for others, while others have been hurt before and have become more conservative. There is no "one size fits all".

Because we're not robots. Desire, fear, identity, status, love, shame, hope, friendships, a sense of belonging, lived experiences. These are things that don't make sense for ChatGPT to have. It can replicate them with words, but it cannot comprehend, let alone feel them. And this is only but a sliver of what we as humans, mere biological matter spinning senselessly on a round rock, in a corner of an ever-expanding, indifferent universe, have to navigate through in this life.

Because, unfortunately for us, the universe provides no explanation, no guide, no promise of justice, and no inherent meaning. We are left to our own devices, so it's only natural that religions, philosophies, and other worldviews emerge in an attempt to answer those questions.

### Closing Thoughts

As I have concluded in a [previous article](https://andreigue.github.io/independence-brings-controversy/) as well, I advocate for humility.

Nobody knows what they're doing.

Lots of people confidently and unapologetically claim to know. But they're either pretending, or they believe in something so strongly that it becomes their reality. It becomes a lens through which they view life.

And that's okay. Because there is no one right answer. So as long as your boat floats, keep it. 

But I'd prefer to say "I don't know, and I'm okay with that". It sounds absurd to some people, but I think it's pretty cool.
